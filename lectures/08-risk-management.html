Risk management

Identify, analyse, prioritise, plan, monitor: turning uncertainty into a managed budget. Every worry gets a probability and a price, and arithmetic (not instinct) decides which worries are worth paying to remove.

01What exactly is a risk?

Exam text

A risk is an uncertain future event or condition that, if it occurs, affects one or more project objectives: cost, schedule, scope or quality.

Pressman: "Risks are potential problems that might affect the successful completion of a software project."

Uncertainty

The event may or may not happen: 0 < P < 1. A certainty is not a risk; it is a constraint.

Loss (or gain)

If it happens there is a consequence: rework cost, delay, lost reputation, safety impact.

Time-boundedness

Every risk has a window. Once the window closes the risk is retired; if it fires it becomes an issue.

TermStatusManaged by
RiskMay happen (P < 1)RMMM plan, contingency reserve
IssueHas happened (P = 1)Corrective action, issue log
ConstraintKnown and fixedPlan around it (scope / design)
Exam text: the one formula to remember

Risk Exposure RE = P × I

P = probability the risk occurs; I = cost of the consequence. Example: a 60% chance the payment-gateway vendor deprecates its API, costing $40,000 of rework → RE = 0.60 × $40,000 = $24,000 of budget that must be reserved.

Practice: risk, issue or constraint?

The senior Flutter developer might accept an offer abroad before launch.

The payment gateway has been down since this morning.

The client says the app must run on Android 9 and above.

BTRC may change SMS registration rules during the project.

The total budget is fixed at BDT 40 lakh by contract.

Tools: risk register templates, PMBOK 7 and ISO 31000 vocabulary, Jira and Azure DevOps risk work-item types. Every ISO-27001 or CMMI-appraised software house keeps a living risk register per project.

02Risk taxonomy: naming the thing you fear

Project risks

Threaten the plan: schedule, budget, staffing.

Late requirements, attrition, unrealistic deadline. Owner: project manager.

Technical risks

Threaten the product being built.

Integration, performance, new or unproven technology. Owner: architect / tech lead.

Business risks

Threaten the viability of the product.

No market, lost sponsor, regulation, wrong product. Owner: product owner / sponsor.

By predictability (Pressman)

Known risks

Found by reviewing the plan, the environment and the technology.

An unrealistic delivery date.

Predictable risks

Extrapolated from past project experience.

Staff turnover, poor vendor communication.

Unpredictable risks

Genuinely unforeseeable. They are why a management reserve exists at all.

03Reactive vs proactive, and the four-step framework

Reactive: "fire-fighting"

The team reacts only once the risk has struck. Mitigation: throw extra resources at the damage. Fix-on-failure: resources found under pressure. Crisis management: the most expensive mode.

Proactive: "risk-driven"

Formal risk analysis before work begins. Root causes corrected, not just symptoms. Risk sources beyond the software are examined. Builds the organisational skill to manage change. Cheaper: prevention beats correction every time.

StepQuestionActivities
1 IdentificationWhat could go wrong?Checklists, brainstorming, pre-mortem, causal mapping
2 Analysis and prioritisationHow likely, how bad?Assign P and I, compute RE, rank and draw the cut-off line
3 Risk planning (RMMM)What will we do?Avoid, reduce, transfer or accept; define triggers and owners
4 Monitoring and controlWhere do we stand?Track indicators, re-score each sprint, retire and add risks

05Step 2: risk projection and the probability-impact matrix

Risk projection (risk estimation) rates every risk in two dimensions, then combines them. Four activities: (1) establish a probability scale, (2) define the consequences, (3) estimate impact on project and product, (4) note the accuracy of the estimate so nobody over-trusts it.

Exam traps
  • Never use words alone: "likely" means 0.3 to 0.9 to different people. Always attach the number.
  • Bands must be calibrated to project size: $80k is catastrophic on a $480k project, noise on a $50 M one.
  • Rank by RE, not by probability alone and not by impact alone.

RE is an expected value in dollars (or person-days). Summing RE across all open risks gives the contingency reserve the project should hold.

The probability-impact matrix (risk heat map)

Escalate / avoid

Re-plan the project. Change the approach, the scope or the contract: do not simply hope.

Mitigate

Fund a response now. Named owner, budget, trigger and deadline in the RMMM plan.

Monitor actively

No spend yet, but a defined trigger and a re-score every sprint.

Accept

Log it, review periodically. Spending here wastes money better used elsewhere.

Management concern line

Draw a cut-off (e.g. RE > $20,000). Everything above it gets a funded plan; everything below is monitored. Steering groups approve the line once, then debate only what crosses it.

Exam text: RMMM, the three questions every top risk must answer

Mitigation: how do we reduce the probability or the impact? Concrete, funded, owned actions with a deadline.
Monitoring: what indicator tells us the risk is becoming real? Define the metric and the trigger value.
Management: what do we do if it fires anyway? The contingency plan and the reserve that funds it.

Mitigation example

"Run a 2-week integration spike against a FHIR sandbox in Sprint 0."

Monitoring example

"Unmapped HL7 segments per sprint > 5 → escalate to the steering group."

Management example

"Ship an adapter layer plus manual CSV import; draw $12k from reserve."

Exam trap

Mitigation happens before the risk fires (reduce P or I). Management (contingency) happens after it fires. Monitoring is the trigger that tells you which one you are in. The RMMM plan is a contractual deliverable in government and defence procurement.

07Assessing impact: a worked calculation

Scenario: a component-reuse risk

The plan assumes 60 existing components can be reused. Local data from three earlier projects suggest reuse rates rarely exceed 70%. Anything that cannot be reused must be written from scratch.

  1. Risk identification: only 70% of the components scheduled for reuse will be integrated; the rest must be custom-developed.
  2. Risk probability: 80%, judged "very high" from the organisation's own reuse history.
  3. Risk impact: 60 planned × 30% shortfall = 18 components to build. Average component = 100 LOC; local cost = $14.00 per LOC. I = 18 × 100 × $14.00 = $25,200.
  4. Risk exposure: RE = 0.80 × $25,200 = $20,160.
  5. Interpretation: $20,160 is added to the contingency reserve. If mitigation costs less than the reduction it buys, fund the mitigation instead.

08Four ways to answer a risk, and risk reduction leverage

StrategyWhat it doesExamplesIts cost
AvoidEliminate the risk by changing the planDrop or defer the risky feature; choose a proven technology; re-scope the releaseYou give up the value the risky option offered
Reduce / mitigateLower the probability, the impact, or bothIntegration spike, prototype, pair programming, automated regression suite, staged rollout, redundancyThe mitigation spend. Justify it with RRL
TransferMove the consequence to a party better able to bear itFixed-price subcontract, insurance, managed cloud service with an SLA, warranty clauses, escrowThe premium; transfer is never total
AcceptConsciously carry the risk with a reserve behind itLow-exposure risks, or risks whose mitigation costs more than it saves. Passive: monitor only. Active: fund a reserveNone up front, but it must be a decision, not an oversight
Escalate (plus one)The risk sits outside the project's authorityA regulatory change, a corporate vendor decision: the sponsor or programme board owns the responseStays on the register, but the project no longer owns it
Two traps

Residual risk: what remains after mitigation; it must be re-scored, not forgotten. Secondary risk: the new risk your response itself creates (an outsourcing contract introduces vendor-dependency risk).

Practice: name the response

Use a managed payment gateway with an SLA instead of running our own payment servers.

Drop the experimental voice-ordering feature from release 1.

Run a two-week integration spike against the bank's sandbox in sprint 0.

A 10% chance of a BDT 20,000 hosting overrun: log it and watch the bill.

A new government data-residency law: hand it to the steering board.

Risk reduction leverage (RRL)

Exam text

RRL = (REbefore − REafter) ÷ cost of the risk-reduction action

RRL > 1.00 → the control pays for itself: fund it. RRL ≤ 1.00 → it costs more than it saves: accept the risk, or find a cheaper control.

Classic: a fire in the server room

Before: 0.20 × $20,000 = $4,000
After alarm: 0.05 × $20,000 = $1,000
Alarm costs $1,500
RRL = 3,000 / 1,500 = 2.00

Every $1 spent on the alarm removes $2 of expected loss.

Modern: regression escaping to production

Before: 0.50 × $30,000 = $15,000
After: 0.12 × $30,000 = $3,600
Contract tests + canary: $7,000
RRL = 11,400 / 7,000 = 1.63

Worth doing. Mitigation may cut impact instead of probability; the formula is unchanged.
Calculator: RRLSlide 14
Limitation

RRL assumes the loss is expressible in money. Safety, privacy and reputational harm need a qualitative override: some risks are mitigated at RRL < 1 because the consequence is unacceptable at any probability.

10Monte Carlo simulation for schedule and cost risk

A plan that says "9 months" is one sample from a distribution. Monte Carlo runs the schedule thousands of times, each time drawing task durations at random from their estimate ranges, and reports how often each finish date occurs.

1 Three-point estimate per task→2 Choose a distribution (triangular or PERT-beta); note correlations→3 Run 10,000+ trials, roll up the critical path each time→4 Read the S-curve: commit to P80, not P50; hold the difference as reserve
Live simulation: triangular schedule riskSlide 16

Slide inputs: O = 8.0, ML = 9.0, P = 13.0 months, triangular, 200,000 trials. Results: the baseline plan (9.0 months) has only about 20% confidence; P50 = 9.8 months (a coin flip); P80 = 11.0 months (commit to this). Tools: @RISK, Oracle Primavera Risk Analysis, Safran Risk, Python (NumPy/SciPy), Excel.

The modern risk engineer's toolbox

FMEA / RPN

Severity × Occurrence × Detection = Risk Priority Number. Attack the highest RPN first.

Bow-tie analysis

Threats → top event → consequences, with preventive barriers on the left and mitigating barriers on the right.

Monte Carlo and QSRA

Quantitative schedule/cost risk analysis: distributions in, confidence curves out.

Threat modelling

STRIDE, PASTA, attack trees at design time.

SBOM and supply chain

Machine-readable inventory of every dependency, matched against CVE and licence feeds.

Chaos engineering

Inject failure in controlled GameDays to discover resilience risks before customers do.

SLOs and error budgets

Quantify permitted unreliability; when the budget is spent, feature work stops.

AI risk governance

Govern-Map-Measure-Manage (NIST AI RMF): model cards, eval suites, red-teaming, human oversight.

14Exercise 1: build and price a risk register

Problem

You are the PM for a $250,000 e-commerce replatforming project. Cost is $18.00 per LOC. R1 (ST) a key developer leaves before integration completes: P 0.40, impact $60,000. R2 (TE) the third-party payment API is deprecated mid-project: P 0.25, $80,000. R3 (PS) only 60% of the 50 components planned for reuse will integrate; each remaining component averages 120 LOC and must be custom-built: P 0.70, impact to be computed. R4 (DE) cloud spend exceeds forecast at peak load: P 0.50, $18,000.
(a) Compute the impact of R3 and the RE of all four risks. (b) Rank them and state which are above a concern line of RE > $20,000. (c) A two-week compatibility spike costing $6,500 would cut R3's probability from 0.70 to 0.25: compute RRL and decide. (d) State the total contingency reserve implied by the un-mitigated register, and name a response strategy for R2.

Full solution

(a) Impact of R3 = (50 × 40% shortfall) × 120 LOC × $18.00 = 20 × 120 × 18 = $43,200.

R3Component reuse shortfall0.7043,20030,2401Yes

(b) R3 ($30,240) > R1 ($24,000) > R2 ($20,000) > R4 ($9,000). R3 and R1 sit clearly above the line: both need a funded, owned response. The instructive one: R4

Its probability cannot be changed (you cannot legislate), so the plan reduces the impact from $90,000 to $25,000 instead. When you cannot change the probability (regulation, weather, markets), cut the impact.

Steps 5 and 6: monitor, control and close out

Reading the spike at month 5: actual exposure rose instead of falling. The regulator published the draft residency rule, so R4's probability was re-scored 0.30 → 0.55 and a new risk was admitted. This is correct behaviour: a burndown that only ever falls means new risks are not being found.

  • Two risks materialised (R7, R3); both were contained by their planned responses.
  • R5 never fired: guardrails cut the hallucination rate from 8.4% to 0.6%.
  • The pre-mortem found the two highest-exposure risks; the checklist alone missed both.
  • The reserve sized on Σ RE proved adequate: evidence for the next project's estimate.

16Exercise 2: a new risk appears in sprint 6

R9: app-store rejection

During sprint 6 the mobile lead reports that the app-store medical-claims policy may cause the nurse app to be rejected at submission, delaying rollout across all four hospitals. P = 0.35, impact $52,000. A pre-submission compliance audit plus a staged rollout would cost $5,000 and cut the probability to 0.10. (a) RE before. (b) RE after and RRL. (c) Fund it? Justify numerically. (d) Place R9 on the P-I matrix; does it cross the $20,000 line? (e) Recalculate the reserve, given the post-mitigation position of $80,300.

Full solution

(a) RE before = 0.35 × $52,000 = $18,200.

(b) RE after = 0.10 × $52,000 = $5,200. RRL = (18,200 − 5,200) ÷ 5,000 = 13,000 ÷ 5,000 = 2.60.

(c) RRL = 2.60 > 1.00, so fund it: $5,000 of audit removes $13,000 of expected loss, a net gain of $8,000. Strategy = reduce.

(d) P = 0.35 → Low band; $52,000 → Critical class. Cell = Low × Critical ("monitor actively"). At $18,200 it sits just below the $20,000 line, but the RRL test justifies acting anyway, because the control is cheap.

(e) Revised reserve = $80,300 + $5,200 = $85,500 (17.8% of the $480,000 budget), plus $5,000 drawn from the mitigation budget.

Model answer

(a) RE: A 4.0, B 3.6, C 4.0, D 3.0 lakh. Rank: A = C (4.0) > B (3.6) > D (3.0). Reserve = Σ RE = 14.6 lakh (24% of budget: high, so mitigate).

(b) RE after = 0.05 × 20 = 1.0. RRL = (4.0 − 1.0) ÷ 2 = 1.5 > 1 → fund it. (Also: a crash during Eid is reputational, which supports acting.)

(c) Mitigation: pair programming on the scanner and sync modules; ADRs and a module ownership map so bus factor ≥ 2; a retention bonus tied to release. Monitoring: trigger if the developer's commits drop sharply, if they take unusual leave, or if bus factor of any module = 1 at a sprint review. Management: if they leave, the paired developer takes ownership, a contract Android developer is hired from a pre-approved vendor, and lower-priority screens are deferred (MoSCoW); funded from the 3.6 lakh reserve line.

5 marks

Short answer: reactive vs proactive risk management

Model answer

Reactive ("fire-fighting"): the team acts only after the risk strikes, throws extra resources at the damage, fixes on failure, and manages crises. It is the most expensive mode. Proactive ("risk-driven"): formal analysis before work begins, root causes corrected, sources beyond the software examined; it builds the skill to manage change and is cheaper, because prevention beats correction. Proactive follows the cycle identify → analyse and prioritise (RE = P × I) → plan (RMMM) → monitor. Example: a sprint-0 integration spike (proactive) vs overtime after integration fails in month 6 (reactive).

Practice: Monte Carlo commitment

A schedule simulation gives P50 = 7.4 months and P80 = 8.6 months. The sponsor wants a single date. Which date do you commit to, and what do you do with the difference?

Model answer

Commit to the P80 date (8.6 months), not the P50 (a coin flip). Hold the 1.2-month difference as a schedule reserve (buffer), report its consumption each month, and release it visibly as risks retire.
