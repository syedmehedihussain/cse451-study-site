Requirements engineering

Discovering, modelling, negotiating, specifying, validating and managing what a software system must do. Most costly faults start here, so this lecture is about catching them before any code exists.

01What is a requirement?

Exam text: IEEE Std 610.12

A requirement is a condition or capability a user needs to solve a problem or reach an objective, and that the system must meet to satisfy a contract, standard or specification.

Functional

What the system must DO: services, inputs, outputs, behaviour.

The system shall allow a student to register for a course section.

Non-functional

HOW WELL it must do it: performance, security, usability, reliability.

Login shall complete within 2 s for 95% of requests.

Domain / constraint

Rules from the application domain, laws or standards.

CGPA shall be computed using the university grading policy.
User requirementsSystem requirements
High-level, natural-language statements written for customers.Detailed, precise statements for developers and testers.
Practice: classify the requirement

The app shall let a rider rate the driver after each trip.

The payment page shall load within 3 s on 4G for 95% of users.

VAT on each invoice shall be calculated at the rate set by NBR.

Passwords shall be stored only as salted hashes.

The system shall email a monthly statement to each account holder.

A student on probation shall not take more than 12 credits.

Rule of thumb: a verb the system performs = functional. "Within", "at least", "99.5%" = non-functional. A rule that exists outside the software (law, policy, standard) = domain.

02What is requirements engineering?

Exam text

Requirements Engineering is the systematic process of discovering, analysing, documenting, validating and managing the requirements of a system. It is the bridge between what the customer wants and the design and construction of the software.

60 to 70%

of software faults are specification and design faults (Lecture 1).

1.9 vs 0.3

faults per page: specification vs code (Kelly et al., 1992).

about 100×

costlier to fix a requirements defect found in maintenance.

RE provides the mechanism to:

  • Understand what the customer really wants.
  • Analyse the need and assess feasibility.
  • Negotiate a reasonable solution.
  • Specify the solution unambiguously.
  • Validate the specification.
  • Manage requirements as they change into a working system.

03The requirements engineering process

Memory aid

I-E-E-N-S-V-M: "In Every Exam, Never Skip Vital Marks". Inception, Elicitation, Elaboration, Negotiation, Specification, Validation, Management.

04Task 1: Inception

Exam text

Inception establishes a basic understanding of the problem, the people who want a solution, the nature of the desired solution, and how well customer and developer communicate.

Context-free questions to ask first

Who is behind the request? Who will use the solution? What is the economic benefit of success? What problems will it address? In what business environment will it be used? Is there another source for the solution (buy vs build)?

Outputs of inception

Problem / need statement. Stakeholder list. Feasibility and business case. Initial scope (vision / charter).

Tools: stakeholder analysis, context-free questions, feasibility study, Business Model / Lean Canvas, vision document. Industry: project charter and kick-off meetings (PMI), discovery phase or "Sprint 0" in agile teams.

05Identifying stakeholders: the power-interest grid

Stakeholder: anyone who benefits directly or indirectly from the system, or who can influence it. Typical classes: end users; customers and sponsors; managers; developers and testers; regulators and external systems.

Practice: place the stakeholder (Pathao Food)

Pathao's head of product, who funds the project and checks it every week.

Bangladesh Telecommunication Regulatory Commission.

Delivery riders who will use the rider app daily.

A packaging supplier for restaurant partners.

06Task 2: Elicitation techniques

Elicitation draws requirements out of all stakeholders. Teams combine several techniques because each one reveals different needs. Goal: identify the problem, propose elements of the solution, specify preliminary requirements.

Interviews

Structured or open Q&A with one stakeholder.

Best for: deep individual insight

Questionnaires

Surveys sent to many users.

Best for: large, distributed user groups

Workshops / JAD

Facilitated group sessions.

Best for: fast consensus across departments

Observation

Ethnography: watch users at work.

Best for: tacit knowledge users cannot explain

Brainstorming

Generate ideas freely, judge later.

Best for: new products and innovation

Prototyping

Mock-ups or wireframes users can try.

Best for: unclear UI or requirements

Document analysis

Study forms, reports, laws, the legacy system.

Best for: rules and data already recorded

Scenarios / stories

Concrete stories of system use.

Best for: agile teams, validating flows
Practice: pick the best technique

You need the views of 40,000 bKash merchants across 64 districts.

Pharmacy staff use shortcuts they cannot describe; you need to see how they really dispense medicine.

You need the exact income-tax slab rules for a payroll system.

Accounts, HR and the factory floor disagree about overtime rules and must agree this week.

Users say "we'll know a good dashboard when we see one".

You need to understand how the hospital's chief pathologist approves lab reports.

07Collaborative gathering and user stories

Collaborative requirements-gathering workflow

1 Prepare: facilitator, agenda, invite→2 Pre-work: list objects, services, constraints, performance criteria→3 Merge lists on a "definition mechanism" (sticky wall, Miro)→4 Discuss: remove duplicates, add missing→5 Mini-specs → agreed preliminary requirements
Exam text: formats

As a <role>, I want <goal>, so that <benefit>.
Given <context>, When <action>, Then <result>.

INVEST: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable.

Example from the slide

"As a student, I want to see seat availability before registering, so that I can plan my schedule."
Given a section has 0 free seats, When I try to register for it, Then the system shows "Section full" and offers the waitlist.

Builder: write a user story with acceptance criteriaPractice
  • Independent: it can be built without another story.
  • Negotiable: details can still be discussed.
  • Valuable: the benefit matters to a user.
  • Estimable: the team can size it.
  • Small: it fits one iteration.
  • Testable: the Given-When-Then can be checked.

Tools: Miro / MURAL, Google Forms, Teams or Zoom + Otter.ai transcripts, Confluence, Jira. Industry: story-writing workshops in Scrum teams; QFD (normal, expected, exciting needs) in automotive and manufacturing.

08Task 3: Elaboration, building the analysis model

Exam text

Elaboration expands and refines the information from inception and elicitation into a requirements (analysis) model made of four complementary element types.

Scenario-based

How do actors use the system?

Use cases, user stories, activity and swimlane diagrams

Class-based

Which objects and data exist?

Class diagrams, CRC cards, analysis packages

Behavioral

How does the system react to events?

State diagrams, sequence diagrams

Flow-oriented

How is data transformed?

Data flow diagrams (DFD), data models / ERD

09Use-case modeling

Actor

A role (person or external system) outside the system that interacts with it.

Use case

A sequence of actions that gives a result of value to an actor.

System boundary

Separates what is in scope from what is outside.

«include» and «extend»

include: the base use case always uses another (shared behaviour). extend: optional behaviour added to a base use case under a condition.

Exam trap: arrow direction

«include» points from the base to the included case (Withdraw Cash → Authenticate User). «extend» points from the optional case to the base (Print Receipt → Withdraw Cash). Include = always. Extend = sometimes, under a condition.

Practice: include or extend?

Place Order and Make Payment (food pre-order app).

Apply Voucher and Make Payment.

Transfer Funds and Log In.

Withdraw Cash and Print Receipt.

Alternate flows are where most hidden requirements are found. Always ask "what can go wrong at this step?"

10Requirements analysis and domain analysis

System description
what the customer wants→Analysis model
WHAT the software must do→Design model
HOW it will be built

The analysis model bridges the customer's system description and the software design.

Analysis specifies

The software's operational characteristics; its interfaces with other system elements; the constraints it must meet.

Objectives

1. Describe what the customer requires. 2. Establish a basis for software design. 3. Define requirements that can be validated.

Rules of thumb

Focus on WHAT, not HOW. Keep a high level of abstraction. Every element adds understanding of information, function or behaviour. Delay infrastructure and non-functional models until design. Minimise coupling. Give value to all stakeholders. Keep the model simple.

Domain analysis: finding reusable requirements

Exam text

Domain analysis identifies, analyses and specifies requirements that are common to a whole application domain, so they can be reused across many projects.

1 Define the domain→2 Collect a representative sample of applications→3 Analyse each application→4 Develop an analysis model of the common objects
Example: the e-commerce domain

Sample apps: Daraz, Chaldal, Amazon, Foodpanda. Knowledge sources: technical literature, existing applications, customer surveys, domain experts. Common objects: Customer, Product, Cart, Order, Payment, Delivery, Review. Reusable result: a domain class model + patterns (checkout, payment, order tracking) reused in any new shop app. Industry: software product lines (automotive ECUs), ERP modules (SAP, Odoo), core-banking frameworks, Shopify templates.

14Task 4: Negotiation, reaching win-win

Stakeholders often ask for more than time and budget allow, or for conflicting things. Negotiation reconciles these conflicts so every party gets a satisfying outcome.

1 Identify key stakeholders→2 Find each "win condition" (often not obvious)→3 Negotiate and prioritise towards win-win
Example conflict

The Registrar wants 15 features in Release 1 by January. The team's capacity allows about 9. Resolution with MoSCoW: all Musts (login, register, drop, fee view) in R1; Shoulds in R2; Coulds wait. Win-win: the deadline is met and the core value is delivered.

MoSCoW

Must / Should / Could / Won't (DSDM, Lecture 2).

Kano model

Basic, performance and delighter needs.

100-dollar test

Stakeholders split 100 points.

AHP

Pairwise comparison of requirements.

Value vs effort matrix

Quick wins first.

Tools: Jira backlog ranking, Productboard, Aha!, Miro dot-voting.

15Task 5: Specification, writing the SRS

A specification may be a written document, graphical models, a formal (mathematical) model, use cases, a prototype, or a mix. The Software Requirements Specification (SRS) is the formal written form.

SRS outline (ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148, successor of IEEE 830)

  1. Introduction: purpose, scope, definitions, references.
  2. Overall description: product perspective, user classes, environment, constraints, assumptions.
  3. Specific requirements: functional, external interfaces, performance, security, quality attributes.
  4. Appendices: models, glossary, TBD list.

Bad

"The system should be fast and user-friendly."

Vague and untestable: how fast? for whom?

Good

"The system shall display search results within 2 s for 95% of queries with 500 concurrent users."

Exam text: EARS pattern and SRS qualities

When <trigger>, the <system> shall <response>.

A good SRS is: correct, unambiguous, complete, consistent, ranked, verifiable, modifiable, traceable.

Memory aid

C-U-C-C-R-V-M-T: "Clever Undergrads Can Clearly Read Very Messy Texts".

Checker: is this requirement testable?SRS practice

It checks for vague words, a number with a unit, and "shall". It is a guide, not a grader.

Tools: Word / Confluence SRS templates, ReqView, Jama Connect, IBM DOORS, Markdown in Git (docs-as-code).

16Task 6: Validation, are we building the right product?

ValidationVerification
Right product? (meets real needs)Product right? (conforms to spec)

Checklist: ask of every requirement

Consistent with the overall system objective? Stated at the proper level of abstraction? Really necessary, or a gold-plated add-on? Unambiguous? In conflict with another requirement? Achievable in the target technical environment? Testable once implemented? Does the model reflect information, function and behaviour?

Techniques

Reviews / inspections: the team walks through the SRS with a checklist. Prototyping: users try it and confirm the details. Acceptance-test design: if you cannot write a test, rewrite the requirement. Model consistency checks: tool-based checks of UML and traceability.

Memory aid

Validation = right product. Verification = product right. "V-a-lidation asks the customer (a for audience); Ve-r-ification checks the rules."

Tools: Jira + Xray/Zephyr, ReqView, Jama Review Center. Industry: formal reviews are mandatory in avionics (DO-178C) and medical software (IEC 62304).

17Task 7: Requirements management, change control and the baseline

Exam text

Requirements management is the set of activities that identify, control and track requirements and their changes at any time as the project proceeds.

Traceability tables link each requirement to its features (product features it supports), source (who or which document asked for it), dependency (related requirements), and subsystem / interface (components and interfaces it touches).

Tools: IBM DOORS Next, Jama Connect, Siemens Polarion, Jira + Confluence, Azure DevOps (ReqIF). Industry: end-to-end traceability is required by ISO 26262, DO-178C and IEC 62304.

The requirements baseline

Exam text

A requirements baseline is a set of requirements that has been reviewed and agreed upon and serves as the basis for further development. After baselining, every change goes through formal change control.

Draft→Reviewed→Baseline v1.0→Change approved→Baseline v1.1

Why a meaningful baseline gives every stakeholder confidence:

Customer management / marketing

Scope will not explode; customers control scope-change decisions.

Business analysts and PMs

Changes can be managed with minimal chaos.

QA and test teams

Test scripts can be written confidently and early.

User representatives

The team will deliver the right solution even if not every need was foreseen.

Development management

A business partner keeps focus and balances schedule, cost, functionality, quality.

10 marks

Practice case: Pathao Parcel

Pathao wants a parcel-booking feature. Merchants book a pickup, riders collect and deliver, customers track parcels and pay cash on delivery, and Pathao finance settles money with merchants weekly. (a) Write two functional and two measurable non-functional requirements. [4] (b) Draw a use-case diagram with one include and one extend. [3] (c) Rewrite "Tracking should be real-time and reliable" so it is testable. [3]

Model answer

(a) FR-1: The system shall let a merchant book a pickup with parcel weight, address and COD amount. FR-2: The system shall show the customer the parcel's current status and the rider's location. NFR-1: The tracking page shall update the rider's location at least every 30 s. NFR-2: The booking API shall respond within 2 s for 95% of requests with 5,000 concurrent merchants.

(b) Actors: Merchant, Rider, Customer, Finance. Use cases: Book Pickup, Collect Parcel, Deliver Parcel, Track Parcel, Settle Payment. Deliver Parcel «include» Collect COD (always for COD parcels). Report Failed Delivery «extend» Deliver Parcel (only when the customer is absent). Draw the system boundary box with actors outside.

(c) "When a rider's location changes, the tracking page shall show the new position within 30 s for 95% of updates" and "The tracking service shall be available 99.5% of the time between 8 am and 10 pm." Vague words (real-time, reliable) are replaced by numbers that a test can check.

5 marks

Practice: validation vs verification

A hospital system passes every test in its SRS, but doctors refuse to use it because it needs 9 screens to order a blood test. Was this a validation or a verification failure? Explain and say how it could have been caught.

Model answer

A validation failure. Verification asks "are we building the product right?" (it conforms to the spec), and it passed. Validation asks "are we building the right product?" (it meets real needs), and it failed. It could have been caught with prototyping (doctors try the order flow early), observation of doctors at work, and an SRS review with a usability requirement such as "a doctor shall order a blood test in at most 3 screens and 30 s".
