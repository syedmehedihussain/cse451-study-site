01What is software project management?

Exam text

Software project management is the discipline of planning, estimating, scheduling, monitoring and controlling software work so the product is delivered on time, within budget and at the agreed quality.

Goal of planning: a pragmatic strategy for controlling, tracking and monitoring a complex technical project.

The 4 P's of management

People

Stakeholders, team leaders and the team: the most important success factor.

Product

Scope, objectives, functions and constraints must be defined before estimating.

Process

The framework chosen to build it: waterfall, incremental, Scrum, XP, hybrid.

Project

Plan, estimate, schedule, track and control the work and its risks.

Why are software projects late?

Unrealistic expectations: deadline set by someone outside the development group; changing requirements not reflected in the schedule. Unforeseen difficulties: technical, and human (turnover, illness, conflict). Estimation errors: honest underestimates; risks not considered at the start. Management failures: miscommunication; failure to recognise slippage and act.

"How does a project get to be a year late? One day at a time." (Fred Brooks). Small daily slips compound.

Project planning workflow

1 Establish scope→2 Determine feasibility→3 Analyze risks→4 Define resources→5 Estimate cost and effort→6 Develop schedule→7 Track and control↻ variance → re-plan

Scope: functions, performance, constraints, interfaces, bounded and unambiguous. Feasibility: technical, financial, time and resource. Estimation: decompose and use ≥ 2 techniques. Schedule: task set → task network → timeline (Gantt) with milestones. Tracking: status meetings, milestone checks, EVA. Planning is iterative.

Memory aid

S-F-R-R-E-S-T: "Some Friends Really Rarely Eat Spicy Tehari".

02Estimation principles and the cone of uncertainty

1 Understand the scope

You cannot estimate what you have not defined.

2 Decompose

Estimate small pieces; errors partly cancel out.

3 Use historical metrics

Past productivity (LOC/pm, FP/pm, velocity) anchors estimates.

4 Use ≥ 2 techniques

Compare results; investigate and reconcile large gaps.

5 Accept uncertainty

Give a range with confidence, not a single number.

Resources to define before estimating

People

Skills required (Android, DevOps), organisational position, location (on-site / remote), number of people (derived after effort is estimated).

Reusable software

Off-the-shelf: reuse as is (payment SDK). Full-experience: similar to past work, low modification risk. Partial-experience: related past work, needs substantial change, higher risk. New components: built from scratch.

Environment

Workstations, cloud (AWS/Azure/GCP), CI/CD, test devices, IDEs, licences. Book early: lead times cause delays.

Each resource is described by: description, availability, time needed, duration of use.

Exam text: formulas

S = (Sopt + 4·Sm + Spess) / 6
Effort (pm) = Σ S ÷ (LOC / pm)
Cost = Effort × rate

Break the scope into functions, estimate optimistic, most-likely and pessimistic LOC for each, and combine with the beta (PERT) weighting: the most likely counts 4×. Use for early bids and legacy re-engineering. Limits: language dependent; penalises concise, well-designed code.

04Function-point based estimation

FP (Albrecht, 1979) measures delivered functionality from the user's view, independent of programming language. The counting rules and weights are the same as in Lecture 4.

Exam text

FP = UFP × [0.65 + 0.01 × ΣFi]
Effort = FP ÷ (FP / pm); Cost = Effort × rate

COCOMO II: the modern successor

Boehm et al. (2000) recalibrated COCOMO for reuse, COTS, iterative development, and size in KSLOC, FP or object points.

Exam text: COCOMO II post-architecture

PM = A × SizeE × Π EMi, A = 2.94

Exam trap

In COCOMO 81 the size is in KLOC (thousands): 32,000 LOC means 32, not 32,000. A multiplier below 1 (PCAP High 0.88) reduces effort: a strong team makes the job cheaper.

07Agile estimation, Monte Carlo and AI

  • Story point (SP): a relative measure of size combining effort, complexity and uncertainty. Not hours.
  • Scale: modified Fibonacci 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, ? keeps big items coarse, reflecting uncertainty.
  • T-shirt sizing: XS to XL for epics during roadmap planning.
  • Velocity: SP completed per sprint, averaged over recent sprints.
Exam text

Sprints needed = Backlog SP ÷ Velocity (round up)
Release date = start + sprints × sprint length

Planning Poker: (1) the PO reads the story, the team asks questions; (2) each member picks a card privately; (3) all reveal at once (avoids anchoring); (4) highest and lowest explain; (5) re-vote until consensus and record the SP.

Probabilistic forecasting with Monte Carlo simulation

Replace one "best guess" with thousands of simulated futures drawn from real historical data. Steps: sample a past sprint velocity at random, subtract it from the backlog, repeat until done; record sprints needed; run 10,000 times. Output: a probability distribution. Commit to a confidence level (for example 85%).

Live simulation: Monte Carlo release forecastSlide 21

Slide result: backlog 300 SP, mean velocity 27.5: 11 sprints at P50, 12 sprints at P85 and P95. Your run will be close but not identical, because it is random.

AI-assisted estimation and planning

Historical data (Jira issues, commits, actuals)→Feature extraction→Model (regression, boosting, deep learning, LLM)→Estimate + confidence interval→Human review in Planning Poker↻ actuals retrain

Techniques and tools

Analogy / case-based reasoning; deep learning predicting story points from issue text (Deep-SE, IEEE TSE 2019); LLMs drafting WBS and first-cut estimates; risk and delay prediction from sprint telemetry. Atlassian Rovo, Microsoft Copilot, ClickUp Brain, LinearB, DX.

Cautions

Garbage in, garbage out. Models inherit past bias and optimism. Explain the estimate; never accept a black box. Protect confidential data; keep human accountability.

08The make-buy decision

  • Options: build from scratch, reuse / adapt existing components, buy off-the-shelf, or contract (outsource).
  • Decision tree: each option branches into outcomes with probabilities and estimated costs.
  • Other factors: delivery date, support and maintenance, strategic value (core IP?), vendor lock-in, security and licensing. Modern form: build vs buy vs subscribe (SaaS, cloud APIs, open source).
Exam text

Expected cost = Σ (path probabilityi × path costi)

Choose the lowest expected cost, then sanity-check the other factors.

09Scheduling principles and the effort-time trade-off

Scheduling allocates the estimated effort to specific tasks over the planned duration, so that every task has a start, an end, an owner and an output.

Compartmentalization

Define distinct, manageable tasks (activities and actions).

WBS in MS Project, Jira epics → stories

Interdependency

Identify which tasks depend on others and which can run in parallel.

Task network, dependency links

Effort validation

Never allocate more people than are available at any time.

Resource histograms, leveling

Defined responsibilities

Every task is assigned to a specific team member.

RACI matrix, assignee field

Defined outcomes

Every task produces a work product (document, code, test).

Definition of Done

Defined milestones

Tasks are grouped into milestones reviewed for quality.

Quality gates, sprint reviews
Memory aid

C-I-E-R-O-M: "Clever Interns Estimate, Report, Output, Milestone".

Effort vs delivery time: why compression hurts

  • Effort rises sharply as delivery time is compressed below the nominal time.
  • From the software equation, E ∝ 1/t4: a 10% shorter schedule needs about 52% more effort (1 / 0.94 = 1.52).
  • The least-cost point lies beyond the nominal time: extending the schedule reduces effort.
  • Below about 75% of the nominal time lies the "impossible region": no amount of added staff delivers the software.
  • Brooks' law: adding people to a late project makes it later. Paths = n(n − 1)/2: 4 people → 6, 8 → 28, 12 → 66.

Effort allocation: the 40-20-40 rule

effort
  • Front-end: communication, analysis, design, reviews 40%
  • Construction: coding 20%
  • Testing and installation 40%
Activity (Pressman)Share

Modern practice: in agile and DevOps the split happens inside every sprint. Testing shifts left (TDD, CI). Use the ratio as a sanity check: a plan with 70% coding is unrealistic. Coding is the smallest share: the work is in getting it right and proving it right.

10Work breakdown structure (WBS)

Exam text

A WBS is a hierarchical decomposition of the total scope into deliverables and work packages: the backbone for estimates, schedules and assignments.

100% rule

Children must add up to exactly 100% of the parent's scope: no more, no less.

8/80 rule

A work package should take 8 to 80 hours: estimable, assignable, trackable.

Tools and use

MS Project, WBS Schedule Pro, Miro, Jira (epic → story → sub-task). Used in every contract scope.

11Task network and the critical path method (CPM)

  • Task (activity) network: nodes are tasks, arrows are dependencies (activity-on-node).
  • Critical path: the longest path through the network; it fixes the minimum project duration.
  • Slack (float): how long a task can slip without delaying the project. Critical tasks have zero slack.
1 List tasks, durations, dependencies→2 Draw the network→3 Forward pass: ES, EF→4 Backward pass: LS, LF→5 Slack→6 Critical path = zero slack
Exam text: formulas

Forward: ES = max(EF of predecessors), EF = ES + d
Backward: LF = min(LS of successors), LS = LF − d
Slack = LS − ES = LF − EF

13Resource leveling, critical chain and agile scheduling

Resource leveling

Shift non-critical tasks within their slack so no one is over-allocated, smoothing the resource histogram. Before: peak 5 people. After leveling: max 3 (the number of available developers).

MS Project "Level Resources", Primavera P6, Float

Critical Chain (Goldratt, 1997)

People pad every task, then waste the padding (Student syndrome, Parkinson's law). CCPM strips padding (50% estimates) and pools it into shared buffers: about 20% shorter overall. Feeding buffers protect the critical chain where other paths join. Track buffer consumption vs chain completion (fever chart: green / amber / red).

ProChain, LYNX, Exepron

Agile scheduling: sprints, Kanban flow and burndown

Release plan: 3 to 6 months, roadmap of epics→Sprint: 1 to 4 week timebox, sprint backlog→Daily: 15-min stand-up, re-plan the day
  • WIP limit: max items in progress per column.
  • Cycle time: start → done for one item.
  • Throughput: items finished per day or week.
Exam text: Little's law

Avg cycle time = Avg WIP ÷ Avg throughput

On a burndown, above the ideal line = behind; flat segments = blocked work or scope added. Burnup and cumulative-flow diagrams complement it.

14Tracking and earned value analysis (EVA)

Classic tracking mechanisms

1. Periodic status meetings. 2. Evaluate results of all reviews. 3. Check whether formal milestones were met on schedule. 4. Compare actual vs planned start date of each task. 5. Earned value analysis to quantify progress.

Control actions: re-plan, re-allocate, descope (MoSCoW), escalate.

DORA's four key metrics

Deployment frequency: how often code reaches production. Lead time for changes: commit → running in production. Change failure rate: % of deployments causing failure. Time to restore: how fast service recovers.

Forsgren, Humble and Kim, Accelerate (2018)

EVA measures progress in budget units instead of gut feeling. It gives reliable readings from as early as 15% into the project.

PV (BCWS)

Budgeted cost of work scheduled up to now: sum of planned effort of tasks that should be done.

EV (BCWP)

Budgeted cost of work performed: planned effort of tasks actually completed.

AC (ACWP)

Actual effort or cost spent on the completed tasks.

BAC

Budget at completion: Σ BCWS of all tasks, the total budget.

15Risk management with numbers

Identify: checklists, brainstorming, past projects→Analyze: probability P and impact C→Prioritize: RE = P × C; risk table→Plan response: avoid, mitigate, transfer, accept→Monitor: track triggers; update register
Exam text

Risk exposure RE = P × C
Risk leverage = (REbefore − REafter) / cost of reduction

Leverage > 1: mitigation is worth its cost; otherwise accept and keep a contingency plan.

Risk types: project (schedule, staff), technical (design, technology), business (market, budget). RMMM: the Risk Mitigation, Monitoring and Management plan, one sheet per high risk. Lecture 8 covers this in depth.

Practice: PERT on a three-task chain

Tasks on the critical path (a, m, b in days): X (2, 4, 6), Y (5, 8, 17), Z (3, 6, 9). What is the probability of finishing within 20 days?

Model answer

te: X = 4, Y = (5+32+17)/6 = 9, Z = 6 → μ = 19
σ²: X = (4/6)² = 0.444, Y = (12/6)² = 4, Z = 1 → Σ = 5.444, σ = 2.33
Z = (20 − 19) / 2.33 = 0.43 → P ≈ 66.6%

5 marks

Practice: schedule compression

A project is estimated at 60 pm over 12 months. Management wants it in 10 months. Using the software equation, what effort is needed? What would you advise?

Model answer

E2 = 60 × (12/10)4 = 60 × 2.07 = 124.4 pm, more than double. 10/12 = 0.83 of nominal, close to the impossible region below 0.75. Advise: keep 12 months, or cut scope with MoSCoW to fit 10 months; adding people late makes it later (Brooks' law: paths grow as n(n−1)/2).
